# Sensate guided sessions: research and clinical references

This document explains why the in-app **sensate-style guided presets** are structured as they are, and points to the main **published and professional sources** that describe those structures. It is for **education and transparency** for developers and curious users.

**Important:** The app is **not** providing therapy. Sensate focus is ordinarily prescribed and processed within **qualified sex therapy or medical care**. Use professional guidance for diagnosis, pacing, and adaptations (trauma, pain, relationship coercion, etc.).

---

## How to read the evidence

- **Historical anchor:** Sensate focus originates in **Masters and Johnson’s** behavioral sex-therapy program (*Human Sexual Inadequacy*, 1970), with a **hierarchical** progression (e.g., non-demand touch → expanded areas → optional changes in medium → later steps toward more integrated contact), as summarized in modern clinical handouts and manuals.
- **Conceptual update:** **Weiner and Avery-Clark** (2014, 2017) clarify the **Phase 1** framework—especially the shift from vague “non-demand pleasuring” to **touching for one’s own sensory interest**—and describe **Phase 2** as adding **partner communication and emotional responsiveness** **after** Phase 1 skills are stable (premature emphasis on partner pleasure is a common implementation error).
- **Evidence base:** Reviews note that sensate focus is **widely used** and often **bundled** with CBT, medical care, or other therapy, so **randomized trials isolating** sensate focus alone are **limited**; much guidance rests on **clinical consensus**, **descriptive protocols**, and **narrative reviews** (e.g., Linschoten et al., 2016; Avery-Clark et al., 2019).

The presets below follow this **consensus hierarchy** as reflected in **patient-facing materials** (e.g., SMSNA, Cornell Health) and **therapist manuals** (Weiner & Avery-Clark, 2017), not as a guarantee of outcomes.

## Preset: Phase 1 – Non-genital touch (`SF_PHASE1_NON_GENITAL`)

**In app:** Turn-taking touch with **hands/fingers only**, **excluding** breasts/chest/genitals; focus on **temperature, pressure, texture** for the toucher’s **own** curiosity; receiver notes sensation and signals **discomfort** only.

**Why this ordering and content**

- Establishes the **core Phase 1 attitude** and **reduces spectatoring / performance demand** before introducing more charged areas (Masters & Johnson, 1970; Weiner & Avery-Clark, 2014, 2017).
- **Structured turn-taking** and **minimal verbal demand** during touch match classic homework descriptions (Weiner & Avery-Clark, 2017; Cornell Health, 2019; SMSNA, 2021).
- **Environment** (private, few distractions, no “performance” ambiance in early sessions) follows the same sources’ emphasis on **predictable, low-demand** conditions (Weiner & Avery-Clark, 2014, 2017).

**Key references**

1. Masters, W. H., & Johnson, V. E. (1970). *Human Sexual Inadequacy*. Little, Brown.
2. Weiner, L., & Avery-Clark, C. (2014). Sensate focus: Clarifying the Masters and Johnson model. *Sexual and Relationship Therapy*, *29*(3), 307–319. https://doi.org/10.1080/14681994.2014.892920
3. Weiner, L., & Avery-Clark, C. (2017). *Sensate Focus in Sex Therapy: The Illustrated Manual*. Routledge.
4. Society for Sexual Medicine (SMSNA) patient resource: *What Is Sensate Focus and How Does It Work?* (Baxter, R., 2021). https://www.smsna.org/patients/did-you-know/what-is-sensate-focus-and-how-does-it-work
5. Cornell Health. (2019). Sensate Focus handout. https://health.cornell.edu/services/primary-care/sexual-health

## Preset: Phase 1 – Full body, non-demand (`SF_PHASE1_GENITAL_INCLUDED`)

**In app:** Same **Phase 1 attitude**; **whole body** including **breasts and genitals**; **even** attention across areas; **arousal** noticed, not chased or suppressed; optional **hand-riding** framing.

**Why**

- Standard **hierarchical expansion** after non-genital work: tests whether partners maintain **non-demand attention** when touch includes **more sexually loaded** areas (Masters & Johnson, 1970; Weiner & Avery-Clark, 2017; SMSNA, 2021).
- **Not** advancing before readiness is repeatedly stressed in clinical descriptions (Weiner & Avery-Clark, 2014, 2017).

**Key references**

1. Masters, W. H., & Johnson, V. E. (1970). *Human Sexual Inadequacy*. Little, Brown.
2. Weiner, L., & Avery-Clark, C. (2014, 2017). (As above.)
3. SMSNA (2021) patient resource (as above).
4. Cornell Health (2019) handout (as above).

## Preset: Lotion and lubricant (`SF_PHASE1_LOTION_LUBRICANT`)

**In app:** **Lotion/oil** on body and **water-based lubricant** for genital areas where appropriate; **warm** products; same **non-demand** stance.

**Why**

- Masters and Johnson described **changing the medium of touch** (e.g., lotion, lubricant) to **renew sensory attention** and reduce **habituation**; modern handouts repeat **lotion/lubricant** as a **named step** in the sequence (SMSNA, 2021; Cornell Health, 2019; Weiner & Avery-Clark, 2017).
- **Friction/discomfort** mitigation is a common clinical reason to introduce **lubricant** alongside exploratory touch (general sexual medicine education; SMSNA, 2021).

**Key references**

1. Masters, W. H., & Johnson, V. E. (1970). *Human Sexual Inadequacy*. Little, Brown.
2. Weiner, L., & Avery-Clark, C. (2017). *Sensate Focus in Sex Therapy: The Illustrated Manual*. Routledge.
3. SMSNA (2021) patient resource (as above).
4. Cornell Health (2019) handout (as above).

## Preset: Mutual touching (`SF_MUTUAL_TOUCHING`)

**In app:** **Simultaneous** touch; **hands, lips, tongue**; **kissing and oral sex excluded** in copy to limit **automatic sexual scripts**; dual attention on **giving and receiving**.

**Why**

- Described in standard **stepwise** descriptions as a phase that **removes strict turn-taking** while still **holding** the **mindful / non-goal** frame (Cornell Health, 2019; SMSNA, 2021; Weiner & Avery-Clark, 2017).
- Restrictions on **highly patterned** behaviors (e.g., kissing, oral sex) in early mutual work are **clinical recommendations** in those sources to avoid **slipping into old performance habits**.

**Key references**

1. Weiner, L., & Avery-Clark, C. (2017). *Sensate Focus in Sex Therapy: The Illustrated Manual*. Routledge.
2. Cornell Health (2019) handout (as above).
3. SMSNA (2021) patient resource (as above).
4. Masters, W. H., & Johnson, V. E. (1970). *Human Sexual Inadequacy*. Little, Brown. (Historical hierarchy.)

## Preset: Phase 2 – Gentle communication (`SF_PHASE2_PARTNER_COMMUNICATION`)

**In app:** **Phase 1–style** grounding, then **short verbal** sharing framed as **information, not demand**; **curiosity** over obligation; reminder to **return to silent Phase 1** if pressure returns.

**Why**

- Weiner and Avery-Clark **explicitly distinguish Phase 2**: **partner communication about emotional experience** and responsiveness supports **intimacy goals**, but **must not precede** solid **Phase 1** skills—otherwise **performance demands** can return (Weiner & Avery-Clark, 2014, 2017).
- Reviews note **Phase 2** is **less uniformly described** in the empirical literature than Phase 1 (Avery-Clark et al., 2019).

**Key references**

1. Weiner, L., & Avery-Clark, C. (2014). Sensate focus: Clarifying the Masters and Johnson model. *Sexual and Relationship Therapy*, *29*(3), 307–319.
2. Weiner, L., & Avery-Clark, C. (2017). *Sensate Focus in Sex Therapy: The Illustrated Manual*. Routledge.
3. Avery-Clark, C., Weiner, L., & Adams-Clark, A. (2019). Sensate focus for sexual concerns: An updated, critical literature review. *Current Sexual Health Reports*, *11*, 84–94.
4. Kleinplatz, P. J., & Ménard, A. D. (2007). Building blocks toward optimal sexuality. *Families in Society*, *88*(1), 72–78. (Cited in Weiner & Avery-Clark, 2014, re: “optimal sexuality” goals alongside structured exercises.)

## Cross-cutting literature reviews (limitations and use)

- Linschoten, M., Weiner, L., & Avery-Clark, C. (2016). Sensate focus: A critical literature review. *Sexual and Relationship Therapy*, *31*(2), 230–247.
- Avery-Clark, C., Weiner, L., & Adams-Clark, A. (2019). Sensate focus for sexual concerns: An updated, critical literature review. *Current Sexual Health Reports*, *11*, 84–94.

These emphasize **widespread clinical use**, **heterogeneity** of what “sensate focus” means in studies, and the need for **clear operational definitions** and **better controlled trials**.
